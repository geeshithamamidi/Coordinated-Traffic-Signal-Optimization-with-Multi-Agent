\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Classical Signal Control}
Fixed-time control assigns each phase a precomputed green split and cycle length; Webster's method derives the cycle that minimizes delay from observed flow ratios~\cite{webster1958}. Adaptive systems such as SCOOT~\cite{hunt1981scoot} and SCATS~\cite{sims1980scats} adjust splits, cycles and offsets online from loop detectors and are deployed in many cities, but they rely on hand-designed traffic models and tuning. Max-Pressure~\cite{varaiya2013max} picks, at each intersection, the phase whose served movements have the largest difference between upstream and downstream queues, and is provably throughput-optimal under idealized assumptions. It needs no training and only local information, which makes it the strongest non-learning baseline in our study; we implement it ourselves on top of sumo-rl and compare every learned controller against it.

\subsection{Single-Agent Deep RL for Signals}
IntelliLight~\cite{wei2018intellilight} trained a deep Q-network on real camera-derived traffic data and showed that a learned policy can reduce delay relative to classical control at a single intersection. PressLight~\cite{wei2019presslight} brought the Max-Pressure idea into RL by using pressure as the reward and state, which improved coordination along arterials without explicit communication. These works treat each intersection's controller as one agent and do not isolate how much explicit coordination contributes. We reuse their insight that local queue information is a strong signal and keep a local waiting-time reward as our base.

\subsection{Multi-Agent RL for Signal Networks}
MA2C~\cite{chu2020ma2c} trains decentralized advantage actor-critic agents that include neighbors' states in their observation and use a spatially discounted reward, which stabilizes learning in large networks. CoLight~\cite{wei2019colight} learns which neighbors matter with graph attention, and MPLight~\cite{chen2020mplight} scales a shared pressure-based policy to thousands of intersections through parameter sharing. Each paper proposes a full method and compares it as a whole, so the separate effects of parameter sharing, neighbor observations and neighbor rewards remain mixed together. Our study varies these ingredients one at a time on a common environment.

\subsection{Benchmarks and Tools}
RESCO~\cite{ault2021resco} standardizes scenarios, including grid4x4, arterial4x4, Cologne and Ingolstadt, and reports that results across prior traffic RL papers are hard to compare because of inconsistent settings. The survey by Wei et al.~\cite{wei2019survey} reviews classical and learning-based signal control and highlights the same reproducibility issue. We use SUMO~\cite{lopez2018sumo} through the sumo-rl interface~\cite{sumorl} with RESCO networks, one environment factory for every method, and fixed train, validation and test seeds.

\subsection{Learning Algorithms}
DQN~\cite{mnih2015dqn} combines Q-learning with a replay buffer and target network, and PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo} optimizes a clipped surrogate objective that is stable across many tasks. Gupta et al.~\cite{gupta2017cooperative} showed that sharing one policy's parameters across homogeneous agents speeds up cooperative multi-agent learning. We use these algorithms as building blocks rather than proposing a new one: independent DQN and PPO, a parameter-shared PPO and a coordinated PPO that adds MA2C-style neighbor observations and rewards.

\subsection{Gap}
Prior work shows that coordination can help, but it rarely measures how much each coordination mechanism adds over strong local baselines such as Max-Pressure under the same simulator, budget and seeds, or how the gain changes under demand surges and unbalanced flows. We address this gap with a controlled comparison of three coordination levels, independent, parameter-shared and coordinated agents, on single-intersection, grid and real-city scenarios, with statistical tests across training seeds.

\section{Environment and Scenarios}
\label{sec:env}
We simulate traffic with SUMO~\cite{lopez2018sumo} version 1.27.1 through the SUMO-RL~\cite{sumorl} interface, version 1.4.5, which exposes each signalized intersection as an agent in a PettingZoo parallel environment. We use two scenarios in this check-in. \emph{single} is one four-way intersection with one agent. \emph{grid4x4} is the RESCO~\cite{ault2021resco} 4$\times$4 grid with 16 agents. The real-city Cologne8 scenario from RESCO is planned for the final report.

Each episode simulates 3600~s of traffic. Besides the default RESCO route files, we generate demand files with SUMO's \texttt{randomTrips} using fixed seeds, with separate train and test files (test seeds offset by 1000). Light, medium and heavy demand scale the default vehicle count by 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5, which gives 1260, 2520 and 3780 vehicles per hour on \emph{single} and 736, 1473 and 2210 on \emph{grid4x4}. \emph{Surge} adds a stream at twice the medium rate between 1200~s and 2100~s of the episode, and \emph{unbalanced} keeps the medium total but starts about 70\% of trips on west-side edges. Fig.~\ref{fig:demand} shows the departure profiles. Vehicle counts for every file are listed in \texttt{scenarios/README.md}.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{Baselines}
\emph{Fixed-time} runs the signal program stored in the network file. \emph{Tuned fixed-cycle} gives every green phase the same duration, chosen from \{10, 20, 30\}~s by mean waiting time on the validation seeds; 10~s was selected on \emph{single}. \emph{Max-Pressure}~\cite{varaiya2013max} computes, for each green phase $p$ with served movements $\mathcal{M}_p$ (incoming lane $l$, outgoing lane $m$),
\begin{equation}
P_p = \sum_{(l,m)\in\mathcal{M}_p} \left( h_l - n_m \right),
\end{equation}
where $h_l$ is the number of halting vehicles on $l$ and $n_m$ the number of vehicles on $m$. Once the current phase has run for at least the minimum green plus yellow time, the controller switches to $\arg\max_p P_p$, and keeps the current phase when its pressure is already the largest.

\subsection{Independent Learners}
All learners observe the SUMO-RL default state (one-hot current phase, a minimum-green flag, and per-lane density and queue) and use the change in cumulative waiting time as reward. Each intersection learns on its own, with no parameter sharing.

\emph{Independent Q-learning} keeps a tabular Q-function per agent. The phase one-hot and minimum-green flag are kept as integers, and each density and queue value in $[0,1]$ is binned into 10 equal bins. It uses $\alpha=0.1$, $\gamma=0.99$ and $\epsilon$-greedy exploration from 0.05 decaying to 0.01.

\emph{Independent DQN}~\cite{mnih2015dqn} gives each agent its own Q-network (two hidden layers of 64 units), target network and replay buffer (50{,}000 transitions). It minimizes the Huber loss to $r + \gamma(1-d)\max_{a'} Q_{\bar\theta}(s',a')$ with Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch 32), copies the target every 500 steps, and decays $\epsilon$ linearly from 1.0 to 0.01 over the first 30\% of training.

\emph{Independent PPO}~\cite{schulman2017ppo} gives each agent its own actor-critic (two hidden layers of 64 units). Each agent collects 2048 of its own decisions, computes GAE advantages ($\gamma=0.99$, $\lambda=0.95$) and runs 10 epochs of clipped-surrogate updates (clip 0.2, minibatch 64, learning rate $3\cdot10^{-4}$). Because every neighbor keeps changing its policy, each independent learner faces a non-stationary environment, which is the baseline problem that sharing and coordination try to address.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
Every episode lasts 3600~s of simulated time and agents act every 5~s (720 decisions per episode), with 2~s yellow and 5~s minimum green. Training seeds are 0--4, validation seeds 5--9 and test seeds 10--14, and test episodes use the test route files. For this check-in the budgets are reduced: learned methods on \emph{single} were trained for 30 episodes with training seeds 0, 1 and 2, and independent PPO has only passed a 2-episode check. Rule-based methods need no training and are evaluated on the five test seeds.

We report average waiting time per vehicle, average queue length, average trip duration and throughput from SUMO's trip information. For learned methods the test episodes are first averaged within each training seed, and the 95\% confidence interval $t_{0.975,n-1}\,s/\sqrt{n}$ is taken over the seed means; rule-based methods have one configuration, so their interval is taken over test episodes. Pairwise comparisons use Welch's $t$-test when both groups pass a Shapiro--Wilk check and Mann--Whitney $U$ otherwise, with Holm correction across comparisons.

Experiments ran on Google Colab CPU instances (Python 3.13, SUMO through TraCI) and the team's laptops.

On \emph{single}, every adaptive method brings average waiting time to about 0.5~s, against 7.06~s for fixed-time, so waiting time alone does not separate them. Queue length and trip duration do: shared PPO has the shortest queue (2.33 vehicles) and trip duration (32.6~s), followed by independent Q-learning (3.48, 38.4~s) and independent DQN (4.05, 42.8~s), while Max-Pressure (6.36, 65.3~s) and tuned fixed-cycle (7.07, 71.4~s) keep longer queues. The learned methods also have the highest throughput, about 2490 vehicles against 2405 for fixed-time and 2128 for Max-Pressure.

On \emph{grid4x4}, only the baselines have been run so far. Max-Pressure has the lowest waiting time (1.09~s, against 3.96~s for tuned fixed-cycle and 6.45~s for fixed-time) and the shortest trips (165~s against 186~s and 203~s).
