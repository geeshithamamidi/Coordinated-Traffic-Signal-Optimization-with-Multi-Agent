\section{Introduction}
A fixed signal plan stores one green split and one set of offsets. Those numbers are computed for a demand pattern that the city expects to see. Real arrivals change within the hour and from one block to the next. A plan that clears the morning peak can hold a long queue when a surge arrives, and an offset that creates a green wave in one direction spills vehicles back when the flow is unbalanced. The plan has no measurement of the queue that is forming now, so it cannot give green time to the movement that needs it.

Local adaptive control does use that measurement. Max-Pressure, in particular, serves the phase whose upstream queue exceeds the downstream queue by the most, and it is a strong controller from local information alone. The open question for a network is how much further explicit coordination helps once that local controller is already in the comparison. A neighbor may be about to turn green, or a short extra wait at one intersection may keep a downstream link from blocking. A policy that sees only its own lanes cannot make that trade.

We ask how much coordination helps over strong local control such as Max-Pressure. Every method runs in the same SUMO network, for the same 3600~s hour, with the same decision interval and the same train, validation, and test seeds. The learned controllers are independent learners, one parameter-shared PPO policy, and a coordinated PPO policy. Coordination adds two mechanisms from multi-agent actor-critic traffic control: a fixed block of neighbor observations, and a spatial reward that mixes the local waiting-time reward with the rewards of adjacent signals. The mixing weight is chosen on validation routes and then frozen. Fixed-time control and Max-Pressure are the non-learning references.

The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A single evaluation protocol for fixed-time control, Max-Pressure, independent learners, shared PPO, and coordinated PPO, with disjoint train, validation, and test seeds.
\item A neighbor observation of four fixed slots, so the input size does not depend on the degree of the intersection, together with a spatial reward that returns the local reward when its weight is zero.
\item Selection of that weight on validation seeds only, with the chosen value stored for the later test runs.
\item A comparison that separates parameter sharing, neighbor observations, and the spatial reward, and that scores each variant against Max-Pressure.
\end{itemize}

Code and trained artifacts are at \url{https://github.com/geeshithamamidi/Coordinated-Traffic-Signal-Optimization-with-Multi-Agent} and \url{https://drive.google.com/drive/u/0/folders/0AHZPnMnLpLvkUk9PVA}.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Classical Signal Control}
Fixed-time control assigns each phase a precomputed green split and cycle length; Webster's method derives the cycle that minimizes delay from observed flow ratios~\cite{webster1958}. Adaptive systems such as SCOOT~\cite{hunt1981scoot} and SCATS~\cite{sims1980scats} adjust splits, cycles and offsets online from loop detectors and are deployed in many cities, but they rely on hand-designed traffic models and tuning. Max-Pressure~\cite{varaiya2013max} picks, at each intersection, the phase whose served movements have the largest difference between upstream and downstream queues, and is provably throughput-optimal under idealized assumptions. It needs no training and only local information, which makes it the strongest non-learning baseline in our study; we implement it ourselves on top of sumo-rl and compare every learned controller against it.

\subsection{Single-Agent Deep RL for Signals}
IntelliLight~\cite{wei2018intellilight} trained a deep Q-network on real camera-derived traffic data and showed that a learned policy can reduce delay relative to classical control at a single intersection. PressLight~\cite{wei2019presslight} brought the Max-Pressure idea into RL by using pressure as the reward and state, which improved coordination along arterials without explicit communication. These works treat each intersection's controller as one agent and do not isolate how much explicit coordination contributes. We reuse their insight that local queue information is a strong signal and keep a local waiting-time reward as our base.

\subsection{Multi-Agent RL for Signal Networks}
MA2C~\cite{chu2020ma2c} trains decentralized advantage actor-critic agents that include neighbors' states in their observation and use a spatially discounted reward, which stabilizes learning in large networks. CoLight~\cite{wei2019colight} learns which neighbors matter with graph attention, and MPLight~\cite{chen2020mplight} scales a shared pressure-based policy to thousands of intersections through parameter sharing. Each paper proposes a full method and compares it as a whole, so the separate effects of parameter sharing, neighbor observations and neighbor rewards remain mixed together. Our study varies these ingredients one at a time on a common environment.

\subsection{Benchmarks and Tools}
RESCO~\cite{ault2021resco} standardizes scenarios, including grid4x4, arterial4x4, Cologne and Ingolstadt, and reports that results across prior traffic RL papers are hard to compare because of inconsistent settings. The survey by Wei et al.~\cite{wei2019survey} reviews classical and learning-based signal control and highlights the same reproducibility issue. We use SUMO~\cite{lopez2018sumo} through the sumo-rl interface~\cite{sumorl} with RESCO networks, one environment factory for every method, and fixed train, validation and test seeds.

\subsection{Learning Algorithms}
DQN~\cite{mnih2015dqn} combines Q-learning with a replay buffer and target network, and PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo} optimizes a clipped surrogate objective that is stable across many tasks. Gupta et al.~\cite{gupta2017cooperative} showed that sharing one policy's parameters across homogeneous agents speeds up cooperative multi-agent learning. We use these algorithms as building blocks rather than proposing a new one: independent DQN and PPO, a parameter-shared PPO and a coordinated PPO that adds MA2C-style neighbor observations and rewards.

\subsection{Gap}
Prior work shows that coordination can help, but it rarely measures how much each coordination mechanism adds over strong local baselines such as Max-Pressure under the same simulator, budget and seeds, or how the gain changes under demand surges and unbalanced flows. We address this gap with a controlled comparison of three coordination levels, independent, parameter-shared and coordinated agents, on single-intersection, grid and real-city scenarios, with statistical tests across training seeds.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}
Each signalized intersection is an agent. The network is a Markov game
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{G} = \langle N, S, \{A_i\}_{i \in N}, P, \{R_i\}_{i \in N}, \gamma \rangle,
\label{eq:game}
\end{equation}
with discount $\gamma = 0.99$. $N$ is the set of agents, $S$ is the joint traffic state, $A_i$ is the discrete set of green phases at intersection $i$, $P$ is the SUMO transition, and $R_i$ is the reward defined below. Agent $i$ does not observe $S$. It receives the SUMO-RL default vector
\begin{equation}
o_i^t = \big[ \mathrm{onehot}(p_i^t),\; g_i^t,\; d_i^t,\; q_i^t \big].
\label{eq:obs}
\end{equation}
Here $p_i^t$ is the current green phase, $g_i^t \in \{0,1\}$ is 1 only after the phase has been active for at least the minimum green plus the yellow time, and $d_i^t$ and $q_i^t$ are the per-lane density and queue on the incoming lanes, each entry clipped to $[0,1]$. Density is the vehicle count divided by the lane capacity, and queue uses the same ratio for vehicles slower than $0.1$~m/s. The length is $|o_i| = n_i^{\mathrm{ph}} + 1 + 2 n_i^{\mathrm{lanes}}$: 11 on \emph{single} (2 phases and 4 lanes) and 33 on \emph{grid4x4} (8 phases and 12 lanes).

The action $a_i^t \in \{0,\ldots,n_i^{\mathrm{ph}}-1\}$ is the next green phase. SUMO keeps the current phase when the requested phase is the same or when the minimum green of 5~s plus the yellow of 2~s has not yet elapsed. Otherwise it inserts the yellow state and then opens the requested green. Decisions occur every $\Delta t = 5$~s.

The local reward is the sumo-rl \texttt{diff-waiting-time} reward. Let $W_i^t$ be the sum of accumulated waiting time on the incoming lanes of $i$, with $W_i^0 = 0$. Then
\begin{equation}
r_i^t = \frac{W_i^{t-1} - W_i^t}{100}.
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
A positive value means the waiting time at $i$ fell. The division by 100 is the scale sumo-rl applies to this reward.

Two difficulties follow from~\eqref{eq:game}. Because every neighbor is learning, the transition that agent $i$ experiences changes as the other policies change, so the environment is non-stationary from $i$'s point of view. Because $r_i^t$ counts only the lanes at $i$, a phase that clears $i$ by blocking $j$ still looks successful, which is the credit-assignment problem. Table~\ref{tab:levels} lists the three coordination levels we compare. Independent learners give each intersection its own parameters. Shared PPO keeps the local observation and the local reward and ties the intersections together with one set of weights. Coordinated PPO keeps that shared policy and adds neighbor observations and the spatial reward of Section~\ref{sec:methods}.

\section{Environment and Scenarios}
\label{sec:env}
We simulate traffic with SUMO~\cite{lopez2018sumo} version 1.27.1 through the SUMO-RL~\cite{sumorl} interface, version 1.4.5, which exposes each signalized intersection as an agent in a PettingZoo parallel environment. We use two scenarios in this check-in. \emph{single} is one four-way intersection with one agent. \emph{grid4x4} is the RESCO~\cite{ault2021resco} 4$\times$4 grid with 16 agents. The real-city Cologne8 scenario from RESCO is planned for the final report.

Each episode simulates 3600~s of traffic. Besides the default RESCO route files, we generate demand files with SUMO's \texttt{randomTrips} using fixed seeds, with separate train and test files (test seeds offset by 1000). Light, medium and heavy demand scale the default vehicle count by 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5, which gives 1260, 2520 and 3780 vehicles per hour on \emph{single} and 736, 1473 and 2210 on \emph{grid4x4}. \emph{Surge} adds a stream at twice the medium rate between 1200~s and 2100~s of the episode, and \emph{unbalanced} keeps the medium total but starts about 70\% of trips on west-side edges. \section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{Baselines}
\emph{Fixed-time} runs the signal program stored in the network file. \emph{Tuned fixed-cycle} gives every green phase the same duration, chosen from \{10, 20, 30\}~s by mean waiting time on the validation seeds; 10~s was selected on \emph{single}. \emph{Max-Pressure}~\cite{varaiya2013max} computes, for each green phase $p$ with served movements $\mathcal{M}_p$ (incoming lane $l$, outgoing lane $m$),
\begin{equation}
P_p = \sum_{(l,m)\in\mathcal{M}_p} \left( h_l - n_m \right),
\end{equation}
where $h_l$ is the number of halting vehicles on $l$ and $n_m$ the number of vehicles on $m$. Once the current phase has run for at least the minimum green plus yellow time, the controller switches to $\arg\max_p P_p$, and keeps the current phase when its pressure is already the largest.

\subsection{Independent Learners}
All learners observe the SUMO-RL default state (one-hot current phase, a minimum-green flag, and per-lane density and queue) and use the change in cumulative waiting time as reward. Each intersection learns on its own, with no parameter sharing.

\emph{Independent Q-learning} keeps a tabular Q-function per agent. The phase one-hot and minimum-green flag are kept as integers, and each density and queue value in $[0,1]$ is binned into 10 equal bins. It uses $\alpha=0.1$, $\gamma=0.99$ and $\epsilon$-greedy exploration from 0.05 decaying to 0.01.

\emph{Independent DQN}~\cite{mnih2015dqn} gives each agent its own Q-network (two hidden layers of 64 units), target network and replay buffer (50{,}000 transitions). It minimizes the Huber loss to $r + \gamma(1-d)\max_{a'} Q_{\bar\theta}(s',a')$ with Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch 32), copies the target every 500 steps, and decays $\epsilon$ linearly from 1.0 to 0.01 over the first 30\% of training.

\emph{Independent PPO}~\cite{schulman2017ppo} gives each agent its own actor-critic (two hidden layers of 64 units). Each agent collects 2048 of its own decisions, computes GAE advantages ($\gamma=0.99$, $\lambda=0.95$) and runs 10 epochs of clipped-surrogate updates (clip 0.2, minibatch 64, learning rate $3\cdot10^{-4}$). Because every neighbor keeps changing its policy, each independent learner faces a non-stationary environment, which is the baseline problem that sharing and coordination try to address.

\subsection{Shared PPO}
Shared PPO trains one MLP policy for every intersection~\cite{gupta2017cooperative,schulman2017ppo}. The network sees the local observation~\eqref{eq:obs} and is trained on the local reward~\eqref{eq:reward}. SuperSuit pads observations and action spaces so that signals with different phase counts or lane counts share one input size, converts the PettingZoo environment into one vector slot per agent, and concatenates those slots for Stable-Baselines3. On \emph{single} and \emph{grid4x4} every signal already has the same space, so the padded vector matches the raw one. One environment step is one decision for every agent, and each episode contains 720 decisions, so a run of $E$ episodes and $|N|$ agents uses $E \times 720 \times |N|$ PPO steps.

Advantages are generalized advantage estimates with $\gamma = 0.99$ and $\lambda = 0.95$. With $n_{\mathrm{steps}} = 2048$, ten epochs, and minibatches of 64, PPO maximizes the clipped surrogate. The probability ratio~\eqref{eq:ratio} and the objective~\eqref{eq:clip} are
\begin{align}
\rho_t(\theta) &= \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid o_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t \mid o_t)}, \label{eq:ratio} \\
L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) &= \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \Big[ \min\big( \rho_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\; \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t \big) \Big], \label{eq:clip}
\end{align}
with clip range $\epsilon = 0.2$ and learning rate $3 \times 10^{-4}$. At evaluation the policy is deterministic.

\subsection{Coordinated PPO}
Coordinated PPO uses the same PPO update and the same SuperSuit stack. Before that stack, the environment is wrapped as $\mathrm{SpatialReward}(\mathrm{NeighborObs}(\mathrm{make\_env}), \alpha)$. Neighbor links are the signalized junctions one edge upstream or downstream, stored in both directions and sorted by identifier.

\texttt{NeighborObsWrapper} appends $k_{\max} = 4$ slots to $o_i$. Neighbors are sorted by identifier and truncated to four, so the slot order is the same at every reset. Slot $k$, holding neighbor $j$, is~\eqref{eq:slot}:
\begin{equation}
s_{i,k}^t =
\begin{cases}
[\bar{q}_j^t,\; \bar{d}_j^t,\; \mathrm{onehot}_{P}(p_j^t),\; 1] & \text{if the slot is used}, \\
\mathbf{0} & \text{if the slot is empty}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:slot}
\end{equation}
$\bar{q}_j^t$ is the mean of the normalized per-lane queues at $j$, $\bar{d}_j^t$ is the mean incoming density, and the one-hot phase is padded to $P$, the largest green-phase count in the network. The last entry is a presence bit. On \emph{grid4x4}, $P = 8$, so each slot has $2 + 8 + 1 = 11$ entries. The coordinated observation is
\begin{equation}
\tilde{o}_i^t = \big[ o_i^t;\; s_{i,1}^t;\; \ldots;\; s_{i,4}^t \big],
\label{eq:coordobs}
\end{equation}
which has $33 + 4 \times 11 = 77$ dimensions on the grid. Empty slots stay zero, so a corner with two neighbors has the same input size as an interior signal with four.

\texttt{SpatialRewardWrapper} then applies the one-hop spatial reward of Chu et al.~\cite{chu2020ma2c},
\begin{equation}
\tilde{r}_i^t = r_i^t + \alpha \sum_{j \in N(i)} r_j^t,
\label{eq:spatial}
\end{equation}
where $N(i)$ is the neighbor set and $r_j^t$ is~\eqref{eq:reward}. The implementation returns the original $r_i^t$ when $\alpha = 0$. The candidates are $\alpha \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75\}$. Each candidate is trained with neighbor observations on and scored by mean waiting time on validation seeds 5--9, using a deterministic policy. The lowest mean wins, and a tie keeps the smaller $\alpha$. Test seeds 10--14 are excluded. The winner is written to \texttt{configs/frozen.yaml}; the grid pilot stored there is $\alpha = 0.75$. Algorithm~\ref{alg:coord} is the training and selection procedure. The reported ablations turn the two wrappers on separately: shared PPO uses neither, observation-only uses the slots with $\alpha = 0$, and reward-only uses~\eqref{eq:spatial} with $\alpha > 0$ and the local observation.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Coordinated PPO with validation-only $\alpha$}
\label{alg:coord}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE train seeds, episodes $E$, candidates $\{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75\}$, validation seeds $\{5,\ldots,9\}$
\FOR{each candidate $\alpha$}
  \STATE Train one shared PPO policy on $\tilde{o}_i$ and $\tilde{r}_i(\alpha)$ for $E$ episodes
  \FOR{each validation seed $v$}
    \STATE Roll out the deterministic policy for one 3600~s episode
    \STATE Record the mean waiting time
  \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\STATE Freeze $\alpha^{\star} = \arg\min_\alpha$ of the mean waiting time, breaking ties toward the smaller $\alpha$
\STATE Retrain with neighbor slots and $\alpha^{\star}$ on the train seeds
\STATE Evaluate the deterministic policy on test seeds $\{10,\ldots,14\}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
Every episode lasts 3600~s of simulated time and agents act every 5~s (720 decisions per episode), with 2~s yellow and 5~s minimum green. Training seeds are 0--4, validation seeds 5--9 and test seeds 10--14, and test episodes use the test route files. For this check-in the budgets are reduced: learned methods on \emph{single} were trained for 30 episodes with training seeds 0, 1 and 2, and independent PPO has only passed a 2-episode check. Rule-based methods need no training and are evaluated on the five test seeds.

We report average waiting time per vehicle, average queue length, average trip duration and throughput from SUMO's trip information. For learned methods the test episodes are first averaged within each training seed, and the 95\% confidence interval $t_{0.975,n-1}\,s/\sqrt{n}$ is taken over the seed means; rule-based methods have one configuration, so their interval is taken over test episodes. Pairwise comparisons use Welch's $t$-test when both groups pass a Shapiro--Wilk check and Mann--Whitney $U$ otherwise, with Holm correction across comparisons.

Experiments ran on Google Colab CPU instances (Python 3.13, SUMO through TraCI) and the team's laptops.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
On \emph{single}, Table~\ref{tab:main_single} gives shared PPO and Max-Pressure the same mean waiting time, 0.53~s, against 7.06~s for fixed-time and 0.58~s for the tuned fixed cycle. The shared PPO waiting-time interval is wide ($\pm 0.43$~s). Queue, trip duration, and throughput separate the methods: shared PPO has queue 2.33 vehicles, trip duration 32.55~s, and throughput 2496.40 vehicles, against 6.36, 65.25~s, and 2127.80 for Max-Pressure and 16.28, 56.72~s, and 2405.20 for fixed-time.

On \emph{grid4x4}, Table~\ref{tab:main_grid4x4} contains only the three baselines. Max-Pressure is lowest on waiting time (1.09~s), queue (10.63 vehicles), and trip duration (165.06~s), and highest on throughput (1453.00 vehicles). Learned grid results, the four-way ablation, and both queue heatmaps are absent, so the test-set question of how much coordination adds over Max-Pressure is still open. The only coordination numbers are the 8-episode validation pilot: waiting time falls from 376.07~s at $\alpha = 0$ to 182.40~s at $\alpha = 0.75$, and that is the alpha stored in \texttt{configs/frozen.yaml}. Those waits remain far above the grid baselines, and a 3-seed selection is not in the file.

Three concrete next steps follow from those gaps. First, rerun alpha selection with three train seeds and a longer budget, and store throughput, queue, and teleports next to waiting time, before treating 0.75 as the test weight. Second, evaluate shared PPO, observation-only, reward-only, and coordinated PPO on the \emph{grid4x4} test seeds and write \texttt{results/tables/ablation\_grid4x4.tex}. Third, once the seed-0 models exist, draw the default-demand queue heatmaps and the surge time-by-intersection heatmap.
